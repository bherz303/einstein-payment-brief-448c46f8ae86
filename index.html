Payment capture policy brief · Internal only

The Close-Out Question

We tell every crew to collect full payment before they leave the job. When the customer cooperates and the reader works, that happens. When either one fails, we have nothing built — no script, no citable contract language, and no record of what our best branches already figured out.

1 · The case

What's actually happening

What we're solving
01

Fix our credit card swipe % onsite

More card-present capture at the job, fewer keyed transactions. Company is at 27.2% keyed against a goal of under 10%.

02

Reduce our instances of invoice-later

Every invoice-later becomes one of two things: a missing payment we have to track down, or a virtual terminal that costs us more. Invoicing is the failure state, not the fallback.

03

Build a best practice for jobs that run long or have damages

The two situations where full payment most often doesn't happen — and where we currently have nothing written.

Why 01 and 02 are the same problem

An invoice-later doesn't just delay the money — it changes how the money arrives. When we recover it, we recover it by virtual terminal, which is card-not-present and costs us the higher rate. So every deferred payment lands right back in the keyed number. Cutting invoice-later is not a separate goal from fixing the swipe %; it is one of the levers that moves it.

The goal

Improve our card swipes and reduce missing payments all around.

This isn't a compliance problem with the crews. It's a set of gaps on our side that we've never closed.

A manager facing a customer who disputes the total is improvising — no script, no coaching, and no contract language they can cite to back them up.

A crew facing a dead or broken reader goes to key in the card, or invoices it out later and has a manager send a virtual terminal. The team doesn't know a virtual terminal hits us as a keyed transaction, so they think it's fine. We still need them to know that collecting onsite is what matters most, even when they have to key it — and at the same time we have to drive those instances down wherever we can.

What triggered this

  • Jul 20 — Paul launches the Manual Card Entry — Branch Tracker. Company at 37%. Goal set at under 10%.
  • Jul 28 — Two Critical branches halve their rate in a week. Paul's read: shining a light on it works.
  • Aug 17 — Company down to 34.4%. North Austin still at 95.5%.
  • Mid-Aug — North Austin's root cause identified: they had been running with broken or missing card readers, plus no process for keeping equipment current and working. Equipment and process corrected.
  • Aug 31 — Company at 27.2%. North Austin snaps back into Critical after one week out — now a habit problem, not an equipment one.
  • Sep 1 — Paul rules on capture methods: swipe, chip and tap are card-present. Virtual terminal counts as keyed, even when the customer types it in themselves. The field had assumed otherwise.
  • Sep 2 — This brief. Messaging built; four questions still unanswered.

Where we got this wrong first

The only prior company-wide guidance was one line in the July 2025 FISERV launch: "Still avoid manually keying in the credit card, it costs us MORE! Swipe or Chip!" It never explained why, never mentioned tap-to-pay, and never addressed virtual terminal. A crew reading that could reasonably conclude a terminal the customer fills in isn't them keying anything.

Treat it as our documentation gap, not crew defiance. Every draft in the working doc leads with that.

Correction on the record. An earlier version of this brief claimed we had no measurement for this. That was wrong — Paul's tracker has run weekly since July 20, and the under-10% goal is already company policy. This brief is rebuilt on that data, and the data makes the case better than the original argument did.

Finding 1

Visibility alone moved 10 points, then stalled

Company keyed share went from roughly 37% to 27.2% over seven weeks of weekly, named, branch-by-branch reporting to every manager in the company. Real progress — and still 2.7× the goal. July closed at 36.58% ($1,285,869 of $3,515,330).

Finding 3

Two branches are two-thirds of the problem — and most of it is mine

CTXH holds 69.8% of every manually-keyed dollar in the company — $168,510 of $241,516 — on about 61% of the revenue. Derived regional rates: CTXH ~31.0% against DFWT ~21.3%.

North Austin alone is 40.7% of the company's keyed dollars ($98,237). Add Dallas ($60,227) and two branches out of ten account for 65.6% of the entire exposure. This is not a ten-branch behavior problem. It is concentrated.

North Austin has been Critical in six of seven weeks. At 99.6% the week of August 3–9, effectively everything was keyed.

And there was a reason nobody could have fixed by trying harder: they were operating with broken or missing card readers. There was no process for keeping the equipment current and working. Both have since been corrected — which is what the 48-point drop the week of August 17 actually was.

What's left at North Austin is a different problem entirely. The crews learned, correctly, that the readers didn't work. Now they have working equipment and the habit hasn't caught up — movers still reach past the reader because they expect it to fail. That's a trust-and-habit fix, not an equipment fix and not a script.

What the number couldn't tell us

For five straight weeks the tracker named North Austin as the company's worst offender at 87–99%. The actual cause was that they didn't have working hardware. No amount of coaching, reminding, or shining a light on the number would have moved it — and the tracker gave no way to know that.

A keyed transaction has at least four distinct causes: no working reader · crew habit or distrust of the equipment · customer not on site · customer refusing to pay. Each needs a completely different fix. The number treats all four identically, which is exactly why seven weeks of visibility produced dips instead of durable change.

Finding 4

The metric is dollars, not transactions — and that changes the instruction

The canvas reads "27.2% of transactions were manually keyed ($241,516 of $886,975)." That percentage is a dollar share. Two consequences:

One keyed $10,000 move hurts a branch more than twenty keyed $400 jobs. That's a genuinely useful coaching instruction nobody has stated, and it's now in the crew card: if you're heading to a large move, confirm the reader before you roll.

Weekly dollar-weighting is too noisy to grade on. No branch changes crew behavior by 54 points in a week. Those swings are one or two large jobs entering and leaving a small denominator. A manager can move from Critical to On target having changed nothing.

Finding 5

The answer is already in-house, and undocumented

Leander has held 0–4% for seven straight weeks — best in the company, and sustained. Nobody has written down how.

Houston ran 0.0% in late July. Amanda asked in-channel what the secret was; the thread never produced an answer. Two of my five branches have already solved this and we never wrote it down.

Correction to an earlier version of this brief: I had the 48-point North Austin drop listed here as an undocumented method worth harvesting. It wasn't a method — it was the equipment and process fix landing. That answer is now known, and the lesson from it is different: check the hardware before you coach the behavior.

4 · The cards

What ballot item 1 is actually asking you to approve

Two cards, written to be printed and pinned — a truck sheet for movers and leads, and an expectations sheet for managers. Short on purpose. Neither makes a cost claim or cites contract language, which is why nothing still open blocks them.

💳 Getting paid — when and how
Movers and leads · print it, pin it
When
  • At the end of the job, before you leave the site. The job isn't done when the truck is empty. It's done when it's paid.
  • Multi-day move: collect each day. If you can't, full payment on the last day.
  • Never leave a job unpaid without telling your manager that same day.
How — in this order
  1. Reader first. Tap · chip · swipe. All three are equally good.
  2. Reader down? Key it in. Still collect. A keyed payment beats an invoice every time.
  3. Customer not on site? Phone payment or virtual terminal. That's what it's for.
Collecting the payment matters more than how the card got captured. Never let a job leave unpaid because the reader quit.
Big jobs matter most
  • The goal is under 10% of the money we collect getting keyed.
  • One keyed $10,000 move counts against the branch more than twenty keyed $400 jobs.
  • Heading to a large move? Confirm the reader is solid before you roll.
Before you roll out — 3 checks
  • ☐ Reader charged, connected, logged in
  • ☐ Final walkthrough done
  • ☐ Payment captured, contract closed
💳 Payment capture — what you own
Branch, ops and field managers
Every morning
  • Readers checked, charged, connected, logged in. Know which one is your spare.
  • This is the single control that keeps our key-in rate down. Every dead reader at 4pm is a keyed transaction.
Every day
  • Walk the three accepted methods at huddle until every lead can name them cold: tap, chip, swipe.
  • Review open unpaid contracts daily, not weekly.
  • Any job that left unpaid → pulse on the branch board that same day, with customer, amount and reason.
Every week
  • The Tuesday credit card review pulse is the formal reconciliation point. Make it mean something.
  • Every missing-payment pulse carries a named owner and a date. No exceptions — that's why they go stale.
When a customer pushes back on the total
  • Don't let it land at close-out. Address pricing concerns during the move, not at the end.
  • If it gets there anyway: you get on the phone, not the lead.
  • Position: we understand the frustration, we're within the maximum of the signed contract, and services were rendered. Claims and management follow up tomorrow — that conversation happens after payment, not instead of it.
⚠ The script and coaching behind this bullet are what ballot items 3 and 4 are for. Until those land, this is the most a manager has to work with.

5 · The ballot

What I'm proposing — and the argument against each

Seven items. Pick who you are in the bar at the top, then mark each one. Every proposal carries the strongest case against it that I could make, so nobody has to build the counter-argument from scratch.

Discuss / Suggest is the useful button. Use it when you'd do this differently, when the wording is wrong, when there's an approach I haven't considered, or when you'd agree but only with a change. Write the change in the note — a better idea is worth more here than a vote, and everyone can read it.

1

Ship the two quick cards this week — mover card and manager expectations card, with the target stated as under 10% of dollars collected.

Awaiting

Both are shown in full in section 4. They make no cost claim and cite no contract language, so nothing open blocks them. Every week we wait is another week of crews guessing.

Argument against

The per-transaction cost figure isn't in yet. Sending guidance now and a fuller manager brief later can read as churn to the field.

2

Is manual card entry a line item on the branch P&L? Does the extra processing cost land on branch margin — and should it factor into branch bonus payout?

Awaiting

Keep the weekly tracking; that part works and we want to keep seeing it. The open question is whether the money is visible where it lands. Right now the cost of keying is absorbed somewhere above the branch, so a manager looking at 51.9% sees a color, not a number that touches their P&L or their team's bonus. My view is it should. The fastest way to make this real is for a branch to feel the cost the same week they cause it.

Argument against

It charges branches for things partly outside their control — equipment failures, customers who aren't on site, third-party payers. And we can't model the impact at all until Paul's per-transaction figure exists, so voting yes here is voting for a number nobody has seen yet.

3

What contract language can we agree to use to hold leverage on payment when a move runs long or goes over the estimate? Should we be looking into this at all?

Awaiting

This is a question for all of us, not an assignment. A move that runs past the estimate is the most common trigger for a customer refusing to close out, and right now every manager handles it with whatever words they come up with on the spot. If there is language in our contract that gives us a defensible position, we should know what it is, agree how it gets used, and train it. If there isn't, that's worth knowing too.

Argument against

Reaching for contract language at close-out can escalate a bad moment into a worse one — it reads adversarial, at the end of an already stressful day, to a customer who is about to review us. The better fix may be expectation-setting mid-move rather than a clause at the end.

4

Build the price-dispute rules and best practices — what a manager actually does when a customer refuses to pay the total.

Awaiting

Not assigning this yet. The question is what rules we think should govern it: at what point the manager steps in rather than the lead, what gets said and in what order, where the line is between holding firm and de-escalating, when it hands off to claims, and what a manager is authorized to concede on the spot. Today that's improvised on a driveway by whoever is standing there.

Argument against

Over-scripting a human moment can make it worse. Every dispute has a different cause — damage, timing, a bad estimate, a genuine service failure — and a rigid script applied to the wrong one reads as a company reciting policy at someone who is upset.

5

Do we push the fee to the customer when the customer is the reason we can't capture onsite? Not when our reader fails — when they aren't there to pay.

Awaiting

This is the one case where the extra cost is caused by the customer's own arrangement rather than our equipment or our crew: they won't be on site at the end, or someone else is paying and we have to call them or send a terminal. Those are card-not-present by their choice, not our failure. The question is whether that difference should show up on the invoice.

Argument against

Customers often can't help it — work, a closing, an out-of-state family member paying. Charging for that turns a clean move into a billing argument and a review risk. And it can only be fair if it's disclosed at booking, in the terms they sign, never sprung at close-out.

6

Payment capture becomes a scored branch metric — a QG with a target, not a coached practice.

Awaiting

Seven weeks of unscored visibility took us from 37% to 27.2% and then stalled. Nothing here sits on anyone's scorecard.

Argument against

Scoring a dollar-weighted, high-variance number grades luck as much as behavior. Fix the metric first or we'll argue about the measurement instead of the money.

7

Add a reason code to every keyed transaction — no working reader · crew didn't use it · customer not on site · customer refused.

Awaiting

This is the fix that would have caught North Austin in week one instead of week five. Right now the tracker tells us a branch keyed $98,000 and nothing about why — so equipment failure, crew habit, and customer refusal all look identical and get the same useless response: try harder. Four causes, four completely different fixes, one undifferentiated number. It would also settle item 5 with evidence instead of opinion.

Argument against

It adds a field to every close-out, and self-reported reason codes are notoriously unreliable — "reader was down" is the answer nobody can argue with. We'd be trading a clean number for a dirty one and might just be measuring what crews are willing to admit.

6 · The open floor

What have we missed?

The ballot is only as good as what I thought to put on it. This section is for everything I didn't — a step in the process, a risk, a whole angle aimed at a better problem than the one I framed.

Nothing here is a vote. Write as much or as little as you want, in any box. It saves as you type and everyone can see it — so build on each other rather than repeating each other.

Recovering payments after the fact

Amanda already has ideas here — this is your section

When a job does leave unpaid, what should actually happen next? Who owns it, how fast, what the sequence of contact looks like, and when it stops being ours and becomes collections.

Damage claims and payment

How the two should interact. Our current position is that an open claim doesn't pause the obligation to close out, and that claims follows up the next day. Is that right, and is it defensible?

Before the job — booking and the estimate

Most disputes at close-out started as an expectation set at booking. What could sales, the estimate, or the confirmation call do differently so the argument never reaches the driveway?

Equipment and hardware

Readers, spares, charging, logins, who owns replacement and how fast. North Austin showed what a hardware gap costs before anyone notices it's a hardware gap.

Training and where this lives

Trial Day, manager certification, huddles, onboarding. Where does payment capture actually belong so it holds instead of fading after two weeks?

Anything else

The thing I haven't thought of. If something in this brief is aimed at the wrong problem entirely, this is the place to say so.
